Books of the Conics¶

Composed at Alexandria and Pergamum around 200 BCE by Apollonius of Perga—acclaimed in antiquity as The Great Geometer—the Conics unified the geometry of second-degree curves by generating the parabola, ellipse, hyperbola, and opposite branches from a single arbitrary oblique circular double cone.

Preface & General Introduction: From Oblique Cone to Prefatory Letters

The Conics of Apollonius of Perga represents the supreme synthesis of ancient Greek geometry. In this general introduction and prefatory correspondence, Apollonius surveys the earlier work of Menaechmus, Aristaeus, Euclid, and Archimedes, introduces his single oblique circular double cone, and outlines the eight-book architecture of his treatise.

Book I: Generation of the Cone & Fundamental Symptoms

Book I unifies the generation of second-degree curves by producing the parabola, hyperbola, ellipse, and two-branched opposite sections from a single arbitrary oblique circular double cone. Through the Pythagorean-Euclidean technique of the application of areas, Apollonius defines the fundamental symptom (symptōma) of each curve, discovers harmonic division on diameters by tangents and ordinates, and proves that every conic maintains its symptom under transformation to any new oblique diameter and tangent frame.

Book II: Asymptotes, Conjugate Hyperbolas & Secant Intercepts

Book II constructs the asymptotes of the hyperbola and proves that they are shared identically by both opposite branches and by both conjugate hyperbolas. It demonstrates the bisected tangent property, the equality of outer secant intercepts, the asymptotic coordinate symptom , and provides complete ruler-and-compass constructions to recover the center, axes, and tangents of an arbitrary given conic curve.

Book III: Chord Rectangles, Harmonic Poles & Polars, Foci & the 4-Line Locus

In the General Preface (Preface), Apollonius singles out Book III with special pride: it contains “many remarkable theorems useful for the syntheses of solid loci and for diorismoi, the majority and prettiest of which are new”, culminating in the long-sought synthesis of the Three-Line and Four-Line Locus.

Book IV: Number of Intersections & Tangencies of Conics

In the Preface to Book IV (addressed to Attalus after the death of Eudemus), Apollonius states the exact purpose of the fourth Book: in how many points at most can sections of cones meet one another, or meet the circumference of a circle, whether by simple intersection or by tangency—and in particular how a double-branch hyperbola meets another conic, resolving the polemic between Conon of Samos and Nicoteles of Cyrene.

Book V: Normals, Minimum & Maximum Lines, Subnormals & the Evolute

Preserved solely through Thābit ibn Qurra's ninth-century Arabic translation (Halley 1710, Nix 1889), Book V is universally acclaimed by historians of mathematics as the supreme masterpiece of ancient synthetic geometry—anticipating differential curvature, centers of osculation, and the evolute of a conic nearly two millennia before Huygens, Leibniz, and Newton.

Book VI: Equal and Similar Conics & Cone Embedding

Preserved in Thābit ibn Qurra's Arabic translation (Halley 1710), Book VI establishes the complete theory of congruence and similarity for conic sections and conic segments. It proves the universal similarity of all parabolas, provides the ratio criterion for similar central conics, and demonstrates how to cut any prescribed conic section from any given right circular cone.

Book VII: Conjugate Diameter Theorems & Extrema

Book VII crowns ancient geometric algebra with Apollonius’s celebrated Theorems on Conjugate Diameters: the quadratic trace invariance () and the determinant parallelogram area invariance (). It then establishes the exhaustive series of inequality diorismoi constructed as the solvability machinery for the lost Book VIII.

Table of Contents¶

  • Preface & General Introduction (Heath pp. vii–clxx): Apollonius’s Prefatory Letters & Historical Survey — From Menaechmus, Aristaeus, Euclid, and Archimedes to Apollonius’s single oblique cone; the surviving Prefatory Letters to Eudemus and Attalus.

  • Book I (Props. 1–27 [I.1–60], pp. 1–52): Cone Sections, Application of Areas & Tangents — Axial triangle, subcontrary circular sections, application of areas (, ), opposite branches, harmonic tangents, and change of conjugate diameter.

  • Book II (Props. 28–53 [II.1–53], pp. 53–94): Asymptotes, Conjugate Hyperbolas & Secant Intercepts — Construction of asymptotes, asymptotic hyperbola equation , bisected tangents, equal secant intercepts , conjugate hyperbolas, and finding principal axes.

  • Book III (Props. 54–76 [III.1–56], pp. 95–125): Chord Rectangles, Harmonic Poles & Polars, Foci & the 4-Line Locus — Generalization of Euclid III.35–36, harmonic pole-polar duality, central conic foci (), and the Three- and Four-Line Locus.

  • Book IV (Props. 77–83 [IV.1–57], pp. 126–138): Number of Intersections & Tangencies of Conics — Converse polar tangents, proof that two distinct conics meet in at most four points, bitangent bounds, and pencils of conics.

  • Book V (Props. 84–113 [V.1–77], pp. 139–196): Normals, Minimum & Maximum Lines, Subnormals & the Evolute — Subnormals, diorismos of two, three, or four normals from an arbitrary point, determination of the evolute, and rectangular-hyperbola normal construction.

  • Book VI (Props. 114–122 [VI.1–33], pp. 197–220): Equal and Similar Conics & Cone Embedding — Congruence and similarity of conics, universal similarity of parabolas, and cutting any prescribed section from any given right cone.

  • Book VII (Props. 123–147 [VII.1–51], pp. 221–254): Conjugate Diameter Theorems & Extrema — Auxiliary points, Apollonius’s first theorem on sum/difference of squares (), second theorem on invariant parallelogram area (), and inequality diorismoi.
